## An important adaptation

Ship 30 for 30 teaches the **atomic essay: 250–300 words**, sized to fit a
single screenshot. This assignment asks for **~1,250 words**. These are in
direct tension, and resolving it badly produces one of two failure modes:

- Padding a 300-word idea to 1,250 words with filler.
- Writing a 1,250-word essay that ignores the principles entirely.

**The resolution used here:** treat the 1,250-word piece as a *sequence of
atomic units*. Each major section is written to atomic-essay discipline — one
idea, its own micro-hook, skimmable formatting, a concrete payoff — and the
sections are chained by a single narrative spine. The reader can stop at any
section boundary and still have received something complete. This is faithful
to the method rather than to the word count, and it is the reason the essay has
3–5 substantial sections rather than 12 shallow ones.

## 1. The headline carries most of the weight

A reader decides in about five seconds. A strong headline contains as many of
these as fit naturally:

1. **Who it is for** — the specific reader ("early-stage PMs", "founders pre-PMF")
2. **The topic** — unambiguous, no cleverness that obscures
3. **The emotional stake** — what the reader fears, wants, or is stuck on
4. **The promise** — what they will be able to do afterwards
5. **The scope signal** — "3 questions", "the 2-week version"

Avoid: vague abstractions ("Thoughts on Growth"), clickbait the body cannot pay
off, and headlines that could sit on any essay about the topic.

**Structural integrity rule:** if the headline promises "5 signals", the body
delivers exactly five, numbered. A count mismatch destroys trust and, per the
source, that trust is very hard to earn back.

## 2. The 1 Chip Rule — the opening

You cannot eat just one chip. The first sentence exists only to make the second
sentence unavoidable. Practically:

- Open on a **specific, concrete moment or claim**, never a definition.
- Keep the first sentence short. Under 15 words is a good target.
- Never open with "In today's fast-paced world", "Have you ever wondered", or
  any restatement of the headline.

Five reliable opening moves:

1. **The counter-intuitive claim** — "Most retention problems are acquisition problems."
2. **The specific scene** — "Three weeks before launch, Airbnb deleted the feature."
3. **The costly mistake** — "The team spent six months building the wrong thing."
4. **The sharp question** — "What would you cut if you had half the headcount?"
5. **The number that stops you** — "Forty percent of users never return after day one."

## 3. The 1-3-1 rhythm

The shape of the piece:

- **1 — Setup.** The hook plus the stakes. Name the problem the reader actually
  has. Roughly 10–15% of the length.
- **3 — The body.** Three (sometimes four) substantial movements. Each is one
  idea, developed with evidence, and each earns its own heading.
- **1 — The payoff.** A single, specific, usable takeaway. Not a summary.

## 4. Rate of Revelation

Give the reader something new on a steady beat. Never let two consecutive
paragraphs pass without a fresh idea, a concrete example, or a turn in the
argument. Warning signs of a stalled rate:

- Restating the previous paragraph in different words
- Explaining why the topic matters after already establishing it
- Adjective stacking in place of a new fact

## 5. Formatting is comprehension, not decoration

The essay must be skimmable. A reader who reads only the headings and the bold
text should still get the argument.

- **Headings** every 200–300 words, and headings that make a claim ("Retention
  beats acquisition") rather than label a topic ("On Retention").
- **Bold** for the load-bearing sentence in a section. Two or three per section,
  no more — bolding everything bolds nothing.
- **Bullets and numbered lists** for anything enumerable. Numbered when order or
  count matters, bulleted otherwise.
- **Short paragraphs.** One to three sentences. White space is pacing.
- No emoji in this context: the audience is a product and growth team reading
  internal research, and emoji read as unserious there. (The source guide
  recommends emoji for social-native writing; this is a deliberate departure.)

## 6. Voice

- Second person. Address the reader directly.
- Active voice, present tense where possible.
- Concrete nouns and verbs. Cut every adverb that is not doing work.
- No hedging stacks ("it might arguably be somewhat useful to perhaps consider").
- Short sentences carry emphasis. Vary length so the prose has rhythm.

## 7. The ending

End on a **specific, useful takeaway** the reader can act on this week. Not a
recap, not "in conclusion", not a call to think more deeply about the topic.
The best endings give the reader one thing to do and one way to know it worked.

## 8. Grounding rules — non-negotiable in this product

This essay is generated from a podcast transcript knowledge base, and its value
depends entirely on being true to that source.

- Every substantive claim traces to retrieved transcript evidence.
- Attribute named ideas to the guest who said them ("As Adam Fishman puts it...").
- Cite with bracketed markers `[1]`, `[2]` matching the numbered evidence supplied.
- Where the transcripts do not support a point, **cut the point**. Do not fill
  the gap with general knowledge about product management.
- Never invent a statistic, a company anecdote, or a quotation.

## Self-check rubric

Before returning, the draft is checked against these. Each is pass/fail.

| # | Check |
|---|-------|
| 1 | Word count within 1,050–1,450 |
| 2 | Headline contains audience, topic, and a promise |
| 3 | First sentence is under ~20 words and makes a concrete claim |
| 4 | 3–5 `##` section headings, each making a claim |
| 5 | Any count promised in the headline is delivered exactly |
| 6 | At least one bulleted or numbered list |
| 7 | Bold used for emphasis, fewer than ~12 times total |
| 8 | At least 3 distinct `[n]` citations, all within the supplied evidence range |
| 9 | Closes with a specific action, not a summary |
| 10 | No invented statistics, quotes, or company names absent from the evidence |
